What is Sequential Decision Making?

Sequential decision making involves choices over time that affect future realizations and costs.

Inventory example. Consider a retailer deciding how much of a product to order before customer demand is known. The goal is to choose inventory at every period to minimize costs. Demand is uncertain and between 1 and 5 units. Unmet demand is carried forward.

Purchase costs: $20/unit/period · Holding costs: $1/unit/period · Stockout costs: $25/unit/period


Start with 0 units. Order 2 → demand 5 → carry 3 units of unmet demand and pay $75 stockout cost. Order 1 more → demand 3 → carry 5 units of unmet demand and pay $125 stockout cost. Purchases cost $60; total cost is $260.

Cire · Nadarajah · Pakiman · Soheili

Source: Tutorial §4, inventory example. Illustrative two-period path.

Begin with zero units in stock. Choose each order before revealing its demand. Each reveal draws a fresh independent, discrete uniform demand: 3, 4, or 5 units in period 1, and 1, 2, or 3 units in period 2. These small teaching distributions differ from the truncated-normal distribution used later in the full inventory model. Default orders are 2 and 1. One possible demand path, 5 then 3, gives 3 units of unmet demand and then 5, as in the static fallback. Ordering 6 units initially instead gives 1 unit left over after demand 5. Other runs can have different outcomes. A stockout here means demand that cannot be filled immediately; it waits as backlog and is filled by a later order. Show how carried stock or backlog changes the supply available from the second order. Purchases cost $20 per unit, holding costs $1 per unit per period, and backlogged demand costs $25 per unit per period. Displayed costs are realized and undiscounted, not the infinite-horizon objective or expected costs. This introductory model carries all unmet demand forward at the displayed $25/unit/period rate; it does not apply backlog limits or lost-sales penalties. The later inventory model introduces the manuscript’s bounds and additional costs. New run clears the old demands; Replay same demands retains the completed path so participants can compare different order choices fairly. The latter is a counterfactual replay of known outcomes, not a new unpredictable run. Neither order is claimed optimal.

Start with the decision row, then reveal transition, expected cost, and objective. We use cost minimization to match slides 3–4; section 2’s reward maximization is equivalent with r_t = −c. Periods are indexed t = 0, …, T−1, so the animation’s first and second periods correspond to t = 0 and t = 1. The objective writes the action explicitly as π_t(s_t), equivalent to a_t; the period subscript permits different decision rules at different times. A deterministic Markov policy is the sequence of feasible maps π_0, …, π_{T−1}; it chooses the second order after observing the resulting state, not both orders in advance. The transition kernel P_t can vary with time. The Markov property is conditional on the period as well as the current state/action, which matters because demand differs between periods here. For the illustrative model S may be taken as the integers (only finitely many are reachable from s_0 = 0 over two periods), with A(s) = {0, …, 6}. Each of the three possible successor inventories has probability 1/3. In the cost expression the expectation is over G_t conditional on s and a; E^π in the objective averages the full trajectory induced by the policy. Costs are charged after each period’s demand, including holding or stockout costs in the second period; there is no extra terminal cost. All backlog carries forward without clipping, as in the opening animation. This is the finite-horizon teaching instance, not the later bounded continuous inventory model. Reveal the equation, then reveal its four underbraces and matching labels individually from left to right: optimal remaining cost, feasible action choice, expected immediate cost, and expected optimal continuation cost. c already averages realized period costs; those costs are not counted twice. For the specific reward transformation r_t = −c, the optimal reward value is the negative of the cost value; do not equate profit to the displayed minimum-cost value. Existing methods for solving MDPs

Select a method family to see its main idea and references.

Dynamic programming

Use a known transition model to compute values and select actions. Methods include finite-horizon recursion, value iteration, and policy iteration.

Consideration. An exact baseline for manageable models; state and action growth can make it impractical.

Selected references

  • PutermanMarkov Decision Processes: Discrete Stochastic Dynamic Programming

Value-function approximation

Replace a value table with a function whose parameters are fitted using simulated transitions or model-based updates. Choose actions using estimated future value.

Consideration. Performance depends on the representation and where the method samples or evaluates states.

Selected references

  • Powell (2011)Approximate Dynamic Programming: Solving the Curses of Dimensionality
  • Powell & Topaloglu (2006)Approximate Dynamic Programming for Large-Scale Resource Allocation Problems

Policy search & learning

Parameterize the policy and improve it using sampled trajectories. Policy-gradient and actor–critic methods are examples within reinforcement learning.

Consideration. Exploration, training stability, and enforcing constraints require design choices.

Selected references

  • Sutton & Barto (2018)Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction
  • Powell (2011)Approximate Dynamic Programming: Solving the Curses of Dimensionality

Simulation & tree search

Simulate possible future decisions and outcomes. Monte Carlo tree search focuses simulations on promising branches, then selects the next action.

Consideration. Requires a simulator; the branching factor and planning depth affect the computation needed.

Selected references

  • Fu (2017)Markov Decision Processes, AlphaGo, and Monte Carlo Tree Search: Back to the Future
  • Sutton & Barto (2018)Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction

Optimization-based approximations

Fit approximate values through linear programs, or relax shared constraints to separate component problems. These models can also provide bounds under appropriate conditions.

Consideration. Choosing features, finding violated constraints, and recovering feasible policies can be difficult.

Selected references

  • de Farias & Van Roy (2003)The Linear Programming Approach to Approximate Dynamic Programming
  • Adelman & Mersereau (2008)Relaxations of Weakly Coupled Stochastic Dynamic Programs

Index & fluid policies

Index policies rank alternatives using a priority score. Fluid approximations replace random population flows with deterministic quantities and derive decisions from that model.

Consideration. Guarantees depend on structural assumptions or limiting regimes; these are not general-purpose exact methods.

Selected references

  • Scully & Terenin (2025)The Gittins Index: A Design Principle for Decision-Making Under Uncertainty
  • Bertsimas & Mišić (2016)Decomposable Markov Decision Processes: A Fluid Optimization Approach

These families overlap: learning, search, and optimization can be combined.

Transition from the preceding overview: a broad range of methods exists, yet users still need to decide how to represent the problem and configure its solution method. The motivation of this tutorial is to reduce that burden while retaining the model features that matter for the decisions. Start with the question at the top, then explain why mathematical optimization is our inspiration.

The workflow is to model a problem, solve it, and use the solution to refine the formulation. Evaluate the solution and its quality, then add cuts or revise the model when useful. In mixed-integer programming, the user specifies variables, an objective, and constraints; the solver manages much of the search and reports feasible solutions and bounds when available. The small program illustrates the output available for evaluation; its exact optimum does not itself require further refinement. This does not imply that all formulations are easy or that all runs find an optimum. This introductory analogy groups formulation improvements broadly; the next slide distinguishes refinement across solves from solver operations such as cut generation within a solve.

Return to the lower question: how can we offer a comparable modeling and solution workflow for MDPs, where the output is a policy? We need representations and approximation methods that do more of the work currently left to the user. This motivates the next slide’s construct–optimize–refine framework. The analogy is an aspiration for accessibility and useful performance, not a promise that arbitrary MDPs become easy to solve. The opening question is visible on arrival. A discounted Bellman equation

A stationary policy \(\pi(s)\) chooses an order from the current inventory.

\[ V^*(s)=\min_{a\in[0,6]}\left\{c(s,a)+\gamma\,\mathbb E[V^*(s')\mid s,a]\right\} \]

Current expected cost\(c(s,a)\) includes the demand in this period.
Future cost\(V^*(s')\) starts from the next inventory level.
Discount factor\(\gamma=0.95\): future costs receive less weight.

\[ J_\nu(\pi)=\mathbb E_\nu^\pi\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^t c(s_t,\pi(s_t))\right],\qquad J_\nu^*=\mathbb E_\nu[V^*(s)] \]

\(\nu\) is the initial-state distribution; we use uniform inventory weights in this example.

The approximation result measures

An average error over states.

The greedy policy compares

\[ \pi_N(s)\in\arg\min_a\{c(s,a)+\gamma\mathbb E[V_N(s')]\} \]

Relative action values at each visited state.

An improvement in average approximation error does not by itself guarantee that every new policy has lower cost.
